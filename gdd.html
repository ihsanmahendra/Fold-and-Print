Fold & Print

You get a sheet of paper with wet paint on it and a picture you need to make. You fold the paper, the paint prints onto the other side, and if two colors touch they mix. That's the whole game.

1. Core mechanic

The idea came from the ink blot trick we all did in art class: put paint on one half of a paper, fold it, open it, and you get a butterfly. I wanted to turn that moment into a puzzle.

On screen, the target picture sits at the top with the fold counter. The paper is in the middle. Under the paper and on its right side there are small tabs, one for every place you can fold. You tap a tab, the game shows where the paint will land, and you press FOLD.

Core loop

  1. Look at the target and the sheet.
  2. Tap a crease tab and check the preview. Paint that would land in the wrong place is marked with a red X.
  3. Fold. The paper flips, presses and opens again.
  4. Keep going until the sheet matches the target, then the picture goes into your Gallery.

Rules

A fold mirrors the paper along the crease, and both sides print onto each other, not just one. If the crease is off-center, the part that goes past the edge of the paper doesn't print anything, so you can use the edge to protect one side. Paint never comes off, you can only add more.

You win when the sheet looks exactly like the target. You lose if paint lands somewhere the target is empty (I call it a smudge), or if you run out of folds. On a fail the player can watch an ad to undo the last fold, or restart.

Why I think it feels good

Most of the fun is in opening the paper. There is a short moment of waiting while it folds, and then you see the result all at once. Mixing makes that moment better, because you see a new color appear that wasn't on the paper before.

Levels only need 1 to 4 folds, so each fold is a real decision. I prefer that to a puzzle where you tap 50 times. And when a fold goes wrong, the smudge shows exactly where the paint went, so the player can see why they lost.

How it compares to games on the market

Folding paper is not a new idea in mobile puzzles. I found a few games that already do it, listed below. In most of them you fold to complete a shape. In Fold & Print you fold to move paint, so one fold changes both halves of the sheet, colors can mix, and mistakes stay on the paper. The obstacles (tape, dry paint, stamps) are also built around which squares can give or take paint, which I haven't seen in the other fold games.

Compared with the games at the top of the charts right now, like jam, sort and conveyor games, the action is different too. Those games move pieces around one by one. Here one fold changes the whole board.

2. Gameplay depth

The game gets harder in three ways: more folds per level, harder creases (off-center and later diagonal), and papers with special squares. I only add one of these per world so the player is never learning two new things at once.

LevelsWorldNew ideaWhat the player learns
1–10BugsOne fold in the middle, single colorsFolding copies the paint
11–25GardenGreen, orange, purpleWhich color has to meet which
26–40SeasideOff-center creases, 2 foldsThe paper edge protects one side; order matters
41–60WorkshopTape, dry paintProtecting squares and planning order
61–80FestivalDiagonal folds, 3 foldsA new kind of mirror
81–100Night skyStamps, holes, thick paperPartial prints
101+RemixEverything mixed together, 4–5 folds, bigger sheetsMastery

Keeping it intuitive

The basic rule never changes after level 1. Every new mechanic only decides which squares can print or receive paint. The preview also stays on the whole game, so the hard part is thinking ahead, not guessing hidden rules.

The number of options grows fast. A 7×7 sheet has 12 places to fold, so three folds already give around 1,700 possible orders. In practice players work backwards from the picture, which keeps it solvable. For pacing I would use the usual pattern: every 5th level is hard, every 10th is super hard with extra coins, and the two after it are easy.

Getting to 100+ levels

A level is just a picture plus the folds that make it, so the designer draws the picture and a solver checks that it works. I already do this in the prototype: every level is checked against every possible fold order. Because every level ends with a new picture, the game keeps looking fresh even when the mechanic is the same. After level 100 I would mostly combine existing mechanics on bigger sheets.

4. Design paradigm

Why I think it's fun

Each fold has a small wait and then a reveal, and I think that rhythm is the core of the game. The player only taps once but has to think about the whole board, which feels smart without feeling busy. When something goes wrong the reason is visible on the paper. And nobody has to learn the theme: everyone knows what folding paper and mixing paint look like.

Getting players to Day 3

DayWhat happensWhy they come back
Day 1The first 15 levels fill the first Gallery wall; mixing starts at level 11They get the "aha" early and finish a collection in the first session
Day 2Daily Print (a special sheet each day) and a login streakSomething new besides the next level
Day 3The Seaside world opens off-center folds; the streak gives a free HintA new idea right when the basic fold could start to feel familiar

For the meta, finished pictures are framed and hung in a Gallery the player decorates with coins. It's cheap to build and it means every level leaves something behind.

Avoiding repetition

Each world adds one mechanic, first in a level you can't lose, then a practice level, then mixed with the older ones. Every level has a different picture. I would also rotate level types: quick one-fold levels, longer planning levels, tape puzzles and big poster sheets. Events every two weeks (holiday colors, printing races) give new goals without new code.

5. Marketability

Why people would download it

The ad shows something everyone has done as a kid and asks "where would you fold?". You can see the answer in a second, which works well for fail ads and choice ads. Every win also ends with a nice picture people might want to screenshot.

The first 30 seconds

TimeWhat the player seesPurpose
0–3 sHalf a butterfly in wet purple paint and a blinking tab that says TAPStart without any text
3–6 sThe paper folds and opens into a full butterflyFirst reveal
6–14 sA leaf: blue on the left, yellow on the right, fold, it turns greenShows mixing
14–24 sA lighthouse: the wrong fold smudges red, undo, the right fold prints cleanShows there is a right and wrong answer
24–30 sThe pictures get framed on the Gallery wallReward and a reason to keep going

Ad ideas to test

  1. "Which fold?": three crease options, the player picks the wrong one and it smudges, then the right one.
  2. Slow-motion close-up of the paint pressing and peeling apart, with sound.
  3. A big picture made with 4 folds, with a "can you do this?" challenge.

Why it could work right now

It uses the same business model as current hybrid casual games: rewarded undo when you fail, interstitials after level 10, boosters and an ad-free pack. It is also cheap to make, with 2D art, one main interaction and levels made from pictures. For ads, one fold already has a setup, a choice and a result in under five seconds, so it's easy to make and test a lot of creatives.

Prototype and 4-week production plan

I made a playable prototype with 6 levels covering folding, mixing, off-center creases, tape and dry paint. It has the fold preview, the smudge fail and undo. I also checked every level with a script that tries every possible fold order: each level can be solved, and most first folds end in a smudge, so the choice matters.

For a team of 1 programmer, 1 artist and 1 game designer, the 4-week target is a soft launch build with 60 levels, mechanics 1 to 4, boosters and the Gallery.

The biggest risk I see is that some players won't be able to picture where the paint lands. The preview and the easy first level of every new mechanic are there to fix that.
